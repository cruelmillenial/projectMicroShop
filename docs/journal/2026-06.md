# Project Journal — June 2026

June is where the MicroShop began cohering as a program rather than a collection of workshop projects.

## Early June — From workshop accumulation to a system

The emerging idea was a small, relocatable industrial capability: fabrication, repair, electronics, metrology, and prototyping compressed into a residential-scale footprint without becoming permanently tied to the building around it.

That changed the design question. Instead of asking only where a particular machine or tool should go, the project started asking what common infrastructure could support many tools over time. Power, structural framing, material handling, workholding, measurement, storage, CAD, documentation, and process control began to look like shared layers rather than unrelated purchases.

Modularity became the central constraint. Components should be removable, transportable, replaceable, and useful after relocation. Permanent joins and building-specific modifications would be avoided where practical. Unistrut and standardized mechanical interfaces emerged as natural building blocks because they could serve as structure, mounting infrastructure, shelving support, equipment frames, and later reconfiguration stock.

## Mid-June — Factory logic enters the picture

The project also began borrowing ideas from manufacturing rather than treating organization as housekeeping. A 6S / factory-logic line of work developed around tool placement, visual controls, repeatability, and the difference between something merely existing and actually being integrated into a usable process.

An early Greek-letter process-code schema emerged from that work. The important idea was that a tool or fixture can be physically present yet still be several steps away from dependable operational capability. That concept would later become part of the MicroShop internal-control model.

## Late June — Material handling becomes infrastructure

A Harbor Freight two-ton folding shop crane entered the project to solve a concrete machine-handling problem. Once that immediate job was understood, the crane began to look more interesting as a reusable MicroShop material-handling platform.

The upgrade philosophy that emerged was deliberately conservative: separate mobility from stability. Casters move the crane; they are not the lifting-mode safety system. Stability belongs to outriggers, feet, jacks, chocks, cribbing, load discipline, and appropriate rigging.

That distinction led naturally toward a staged upgrade path involving better pins and fasteners, serviceable caster assemblies, structural tube and plate work, deployable stabilizers, and eventually—if justified—a powered hydraulic module. The goal was not to turn a folding engine crane into fantasy industrial equipment. It was to make a compact handling appliance more predictable and maintainable inside a constrained shop.

## Late June — The room becomes a subsystem

The primary enclosed work area was defined as a narrow residential room roughly 8 ft wide, 16 ft long, and 10 ft high. Because the project operates in rented space, modifications need to remain reversible and mechanically understandable.

The room itself therefore became a design problem: flooring, vibration, noise control, machine isolation, lighting, storage, dust management, and eventually sheltered electronics and metrology work all had to coexist with portability.

Heavy rubber barn mats were selected as part of the machine/noise-isolation floor strategy. Structural framing concepts increasingly favored a free-standing strut exoskeleton rather than modifications that depended heavily on the building.

## End-of-month state

By the end of June, MicroShop had acquired a recognizable architecture:

- modular structural infrastructure
- machine and material-handling capability
- a constrained but intentionally engineered room
- emerging electronics and metrology ambitions
- CAD and measurement as supporting infrastructure
- factory-style process control rather than ad-hoc organization

The program was still mostly a collection of active physical builds and design threads. The next problem was coordination: how to preserve decisions, dependencies, measurements, and reusable work without turning the project into paperwork for its own sake.
