# South Bend Lathe Unload — Operation Retrospective

**Task:** MS-OPS-001  
**Status:** Physical operation complete; retrospective record  
**Document type:** Historical case study / lessons learned — **not a lifting SOP**

## Purpose

Record the planning, constraints, execution evidence, outcome, and lessons from moving a South Bend 9x36 lathe from a Honda Insight into the residential MicroShop environment. This document intentionally preserves uncertainty where the surviving record does not establish an execution detail.

## Evidence discipline

The reconstruction uses contemporaneous discussion, photographed handwritten route measurements, planning graphics, operation/staging photographs, the primary-source checkpoint produced from the transferred `Lathe Unloading and Hoist Ideas` thread, and later crane discussions only where they document lessons that followed the move.

Evidence classes used here:

- **Established:** directly documented or photographed.
- **Planning:** considered or designed before execution; not proof it was used.
- **Observed outcome:** visible after the operation.
- **Unrecovered:** detail is not established strongly enough to state as fact.

## Mission

The mission was to extract a South Bend 9x36 engine lathe from a Honda Insight, transition it through a constrained residential approach, and place it indoors on a prepared rolling wooden platform/cart without injury or meaningful damage.

The problem was not merely lifting a heavy object. It combined uncertain center of gravity, precision-machine fragility, vehicle-clearance constraints, residential steps and thresholds, limited maneuvering room, floor protection, crane reach, and the need to establish a safe receiving condition before committing to the move.

## Concept evolution

The final solution emerged through several design branches rather than from a single predetermined plan.

### A — Vehicle-mounted jib concept

**Planning only; rejected.**

An early concept placed a jib/hoist at the vehicle receiver to obtain a vertical pick over the lathe. It was rejected because the resulting receiver/unibody bending moment, torsional loading, suspension response, hatch geometry, and sensitivity to swinging or shock loading made the vehicle an unattractive structural element in the lifting system.

### B — Timber extraction gantry

**Planning only; developed substantially but superseded.**

A purpose-built timber gantry/cart concept was developed around laminated lumber members, bracing, counterweight, a chain hoist, rated sling, and a sled/bridge. The planned sequence was to move the lathe rearward, rig it, lift only enough to clear, remove the vehicle or substitute the receiving cart, and lower.

Although not the final lifting appliance, this branch established an important doctrine retained in later planning: minimize suspended travel and use lifting primarily to create the vertical separation needed for a controlled transfer.

### C — Route / cart / bridge / cribbing system

**Planning with elements supported by staging photographs.**

The ingress problem was treated separately from vehicle extraction. A receiving cart, wood rails/boards, cribbing or blocking, and controlled transitions were developed for the route from exterior pavement through the steps/threshold to the interior.

### D — Commercial folding shop crane

**Actually used.**

A Harbor Freight / Pittsburgh 2-ton folding shop crane ultimately became the principal lifting appliance. The lathe was successfully brought indoors, and the crane was retained afterward as MicroShop material-handling infrastructure rather than disposed of as a one-mission tool.

## Load and machine considerations

The machine was a South Bend 9x36 lathe. The surviving checkpoint did not preserve a reliable as-lifted mass, numerical center-of-gravity location, or complete installed/removed component inventory.

Planning correctly treated the headstock end as the more mass-concentrated end and emphasized balance, low test lifts, protection of precision surfaces, and avoidance of nonstructural lift points such as handwheels, feed hardware, sheet metal, or similar appendages.

The exact final sling path and lift points are not recovered strongly enough to publish as a repeatable rigging arrangement.

## Risk picture

The principal hazards identified during planning were:

- crane overturning or instability from load moment and reach;
- reduced boom capacity at longer extension;
- dynamic loading from sudden lift/lower, bumps, or swing;
- sling migration and damage to precision or fragile machine features;
- uncontrolled rotation from asymmetric mass;
- crush and pinch zones during cart, crane, vehicle, threshold, and doorway interaction;
- dropped load from rigging or hardware failure;
- caster movement and overreliance on caster brakes;
- damage to the vehicle, machine, floor, porch, or other property;
- limited escape paths in a residential workspace.

Mitigations clearly supported by the record include route surveying, staged boards/rails, preparation of the receiving platform before final placement, conservative low-speed handling doctrine, deployed crane legs, and deliberate concern for keeping the load low. Specific chocks, taglines, cribbing arrangements, and sling configurations were discussed but are not sufficiently documented to claim as executed controls.

## Execution reconstruction

The complete real-time lift chronology was not preserved, so this section stops at the evidence boundary.

1. The exterior ingress route was surveyed and recorded with dimensions and inclinations.
2. A rolling wooden platform/cart was prepared as the receiving base.
3. Boards/rails and support material were fabricated or staged for route transitions.
4. The 2-ton folding shop crane was assembled/staged and rigging was prepared.
5. The vehicle-extraction mission proceeded with the shop crane rather than the earlier hitch-jib or timber-gantry concepts.
6. The machine was transferred out of the vehicle-side starting condition and ultimately onto the receiving cart.
7. The cart/machine was moved through the surveyed residential route and into the MicroShop space.
8. Post-operation photographs establish the lathe indoors on the rolling platform with the crane nearby.

The exact successful pickup sequence, boom setting, sling path, vehicle-clearance maneuver, intermediate setdowns, helper participation, and final de-rigging sequence remain unrecovered. These omissions are historical gaps, not invitations to infer a procedure.

## Outcome

**Mission accomplished.** The surviving photographic record shows the lathe indoors on its rolling wooden platform/cart and apparently intact. The crane remained usable and immediately transitioned into a longer-lived MicroShop material-handling asset.

The contemporaneous record contains no report of injury, machine damage, vehicle damage, property damage, or a near miss. Because a formal post-operation inspection report was not created at the time, this should be read as **no damage/near miss recorded**, rather than as a later certification that none occurred.

## Lessons learned

### 1. Survey the route, not merely the load

Thresholds, slopes, steps, turning room, crane-leg geometry, and receiving-space constraints can dominate a residential machine move. The route survey was one of the strongest parts of the operation record and is worth repeating.

### 2. Prepare the receiving condition first

The rolling platform existed before final placement. That allowed the mission to end with the machine in a movable, stable-enough intermediate state without requiring the final lathe cabinet/base to exist first.

### 3. Minimize suspended travel

The rejected and superseded concepts repeatedly converged on the same useful principle: lift only as high and as long as required to accomplish a transfer. Moving a vehicle, cart, bridge, or support system can be preferable to traveling a suspended precision machine.

### 4. Mobility and stability are different functions

Subsequent experience with the crane and loaded carts reinforced the distinction: casters are for positioning; they should not automatically be treated as the structural stability system for a stationary heavy load. This led directly toward chocks, feet, cribbing, outriggers, and jack/stabilizer concepts.

### 5. Commodity crane hardware deserves inspection

The move succeeded, but subsequent inspection generated a program of work around boom-end pin/bolt geometry, smooth bearing surfaces, keeping threads out of shear/bearing zones where practical, standardized pin families, and improved caster assemblies.

### 6. A one-time logistics problem can justify reusable infrastructure

The crane purchase solved the immediate unloading problem and then became a general MicroShop handling capability. It subsequently generated a measurement register, CAD work, caster and pin upgrade paths, outrigger/stabilizer concepts, and a possible powered cart/crane architecture.

### 7. Record execution evidence while the operation is happening

Planning evidence survived much better than execution evidence. Future nontrivial moves should capture, at minimum, the actual load estimate, boom setting, rigging path and WLLs, low test lift, initial pickup, major transitions, final setdown, and post-operation inspection.

## Historical unknowns retained intentionally

If additional phone photographs are recovered later, they may be added as evidence without reopening the physical operation itself.

## Project consequence

The lathe unload was more than a transport anecdote. It established the first substantial MicroShop material-handling problem and converted a temporary lifting requirement into a reusable capability domain. The downstream crane mechanical-upgrade, CAD/measurement, rigging, cart, and stability work should therefore be understood partly as lessons propagated from this operation.
